    3. Bivariate and multivariate analysis (training set)¶

    Classification EDA, section 4 of 7. Use Next at the bottom to keep reading, or open the full report on one page.

    3A. Numerical x numerical¶

    Choice: Spearman. 4 of the 6 numerical features are strongly skewed (balance, campaign, pdays, previous), and pdays/previous are dominated by a sentinel/zero mass. Pearson assumes linear relationships and is sensitive to extreme values, while Spearman uses ranks: it captures monotone relationships and is robust to outliers. Pearson is shown only for comparison.

    Top pairs by |Spearman|:

    pair spearman pearson
    pdays - previous 0.986 0.439
    day - campaign 0.139 0.162
    campaign - pdays -0.112 -0.089
    campaign - previous -0.108 -0.031
    age - balance 0.096 0.096
    day - pdays -0.093 -0.093

    Figure 6 - conclusion. - The only strongly correlated pair is pdays - previous: Spearman 0.986, compared with Pearson 0.439. Pearson understates it because the relationship is monotone but far from linear (a large sentinel mass plus long tails). - All other pairs have |rho| <= 0.139. The next strongest are day - campaign (0.139) and campaign - pdays (-0.112). Apart from the pair above, the numerical features are close to non-redundant.

    Figure 7 - conclusion. The 0.986 correlation is driven almost entirely by the shared sentinel, since pdays = -1 exactly when previous = 0 (1B). Among previously contacted clients, Spearman is only -0.099 (Pearson -0.016), so the actual magnitudes are nearly unrelated. Redundancy: the two features duplicate the binary "previously contacted" information, not their values. Part 2 should encode that flag once and keep both magnitudes (or test dropping one) instead of treating them as collinear duplicates.

    3B. Categorical x target¶

    The training base rate is 11.70%. Rates for every level of every categorical feature, with 95% Wilson intervals and lift, are in part1_outputs/categorical_target_rates_train.csv. The spread between each feature's highest and lowest level rate:

    feature max - min rate (pp)
    poutcome 55.4
    month 44.2
    job 21.6
    contact 10.7
    housing 9.0
    education 6.3
    default 5.9
    loan 5.8
    marital 5.0

    The features with the largest spread are poutcome (55.4 pp), month (44.2 pp), job (21.6 pp). The smallest is marital (5.0 pp). The selected figures cover the strongest and most decision-relevant features.

    Figure 8 - conclusion. - poutcome separates the classes most. Clients whose previous campaign was a success subscribe at 64.6%, against 12.3% (failure), 16.5% (other) and 9.2% (unknown = never contacted). - contact = unknown has a much lower rate (4.1%) than cellular (14.8%) and telephone (13.9%). This matches the early-period artifact found in 1B, so it partly encodes time. - Clients without a housing loan (16.7% vs 7.7%) or without a personal loan (12.6% vs 6.8%) subscribe more often. - The intervals for poutcome = success, contact = unknown, housing and loan do not overlap with their contrasts. cellular and telephone overlap, so the contact channel itself adds little once unknown is separated out.

    Figure 9 - conclusion. Contact volume and subscription rate move in opposite directions (Spearman correlation across the 12 months: -0.87). - The busiest month has the lowest rate: may (6.7%, n = 11,062). - Low-volume months have rates above 30%: mar (50.9%, n = 391), sep (45.2%, n = 451), oct (43.8%, n = 592), dec (47.2%, n = 178). The maximum is mar (50.9%, n = 391). - Volume and rate are drawn as two panels on separate axes, not as a dual-axis chart. - month is strongly predictive, but given the drift found in 1B it likely mixes seasonality with the period of data collection (there is no year column). Its confidence intervals are wide for the thin months.

    Figure 10 - conclusion. - job: students (28.9%) and retired clients (23.0%) subscribe far above the base rate. Blue-collar clients (7.3%), housemaids (8.4%), entrepreneurs (8.5%) and services workers (8.9%) subscribe below it. - education: the rate increases from primary (8.6%) to secondary (10.7%) to tertiary (14.9%). unknown (13.2%) has a wide interval, consistent with its small size. - These features carry moderate, roughly ordinal signal. job = unknown (12.0%) has a very wide interval and is too small to interpret.

    Figure 11 - conclusion. Why this pair: age and job are strongly linked (students and retirees are at the age extremes), and both have high-rate groups in 3B. - Location: median age ranges from 26 (students, no) to 59 (retired, no). - Within groups: for most jobs, the yes and no boxes are within a few years of each other. The clear exception is retired clients: those who subscribed are older (median 67 vs 59) and more spread out (IQR 14 vs 7). - Overall: the age effect is non-linear. Clients aged 60+ subscribe at 33.7% (n = 1,447) and those under 25 at 25.6% (n = 657), against 9.8% for ages 30-59. The overall medians by class are almost equal (38 vs 39), which is why a linear age effect would be weak. Part 2 should consider age bins or non-linear models.

The figure checks whether balance still matters within an education level. - Location: within every education level, subscribers have a higher median balance: - primary: 796 vs 390.5 - secondary: 651 vs 373 - tertiary: 882 vs 538 - Spread: subscribers also have a larger IQR (for example tertiary 2,317 vs 1,577). - The balance effect is therefore not explained by education alone.     Figure 13 - conclusion. Why this pair: campaign measures call effort, and contact is the channel used. This checks whether extra calls relate to subscription in the same way for each channel, and whether the unknown (early-period) group behaves differently. - Location: medians are equal (2) in every box, so location barely differs. - Spread: the difference is in the upper tail. For cellular and telephone, subscribers have a narrower IQR (cellular 1 vs 2; telephone 2 vs 3), while for unknown they are equal (2 vs 2). The extreme counts belong mostly to non-subscribers. - Overall: the rate is 14.6% after a single contact and 5.9% after more than 5 contacts (n = 3,483). Repeated calls have diminishing returns. The signal is in the tail, so a capped or log-transformed campaign is preferable to the raw count.
